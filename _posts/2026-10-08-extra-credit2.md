## Event Overview

On October 7, 2026, I had the privilege to attend the online workshop Breaking Up with Google Docs: Privacy-First Alternatives for Research, Writing, and Note-Taking. The 30-minute session was led by A. M. Alpin, NYU Libraries’ Librarian for Creative and Public Scholarship, as part of the Open Knowledge department’s bite-size workshop series. The session’s main purpose was to introduce Joplin and Obsidian as alternatives to familiar platforms such as Google Docs, Microsoft Word, and Evernote. That does not mean the session was telling us to immediately abandon all those programs, but instead the session helped us understand what we’re choosing to opt into when using these platforms and the alternatives that exist.

Before this workshop, I mostly thought about note-taking software in terms of convenience. To me, if an application sync’d reliably, was easy to search, and worked on my laptop and phone, that generally seemed like enough of a bar to clear for it to become my main app. But then, the workshop made me think more seriously about concepts like data ownership, offline access, privacy, and platform lock-in. In other words, the hidden tradeoffs when choosing some of these platforms. Generally speaking, using any cloud service can also make a user dependent on one company’s servers, file formats, pricing decisions, and products. This becomes especially important to consider when a note-taking app contains years of academic research or personal writing, which makes switching even harder.

## Why consider alternatives?

To start off with, the presentation opened by explaining many reasons someone might want an alternative to a proprietary cloud platform. Specifically, when files exist mainly on just one company’s servers, users have less control over storage and future access. Offline work may also require extra setup, while migrating a large archive can become difficult. The speaker also raised concerns about features being added without users requesting them, for instance Gemini’s integration into Google Docs. The broader point was that software is not neutral, and that its ownership and design influence what users can do with their own information.

I found that argument relevant to our class, too. The course has already encouraged us to ask questions like who creates a dataset, which categories structure it, what may be missing, etc. And the workshop basically applied a similar critical perspective to our own notes, since a note-taking application is also a system for classifying information: folders, tags, links, file formats, search functions, and interfaces all influence how knowledge is recorded and later retrieved. That also means privacy-first software goes beyond just being helpful for cybersecurity; it also gives people control over the very infrastructure through which our personal and academic information is organized.

## Joplin

Out of the two programs presented, Joplin was presented as the more approachable option. It is free, open source, works across major desktop and mobile operating systems, and organizes material through notebooks, sub-notebooks, tags, attachments, and to-do lists. Its structure resembles Evernote or a traditional filing cabinet, so the learning curve seems fairly gentle. Notes remain in a local database, and synchronization can use services such as Dropbox, OneDrive, Nextcloud, or WebDAV. The session also emphasized Joplin’s end-to-end encryption and web clipper, which can save web pages, screenshots, or even Markdown directly into a chosen notebook.

I felt like the live demonstration was very helpful, as it made Joplin feel less abstract. The speaker showed a large collection of research notes organized into nested notebooks, including a Dolly Parton research folder containing articles, audio, and images that she inserted. Then, she used a web clipper to capture material from her browser and placed it directly into that existing structure. This part of the demonstration especially looked very useful to me for research because it reduces the usual mess of open tabs, random downloads, and links saved without context.

## Obsidian

In contrast, Obsidian approaches knowledge management quite differently. Instead of treating notes mainly as items inside notebooks, it encourages users to create links among ideas (like a mind map). Notes are stored locally as plain Markdown files, meaning they can still be opened with an ordinary text editor even if the application eventually disappears. Obsidian also offers backlinks, a visual graph of connections, Canvas for arranging ideas spatially, and a large community-plugin ecosystem. The application is free for personal use, although its core is proprietary rather than open source, and its official synchronization service is optional and paid.

The graph view was the most interesting feature to me. During the demonstration, linked notes appeared as nodes connected by lines, making clusters of related research visible. Of course, this does not automatically create insight (a graph can become cluttered if links are added without purpose), but it offers a very interesting and different way of thinking from a normal folder hierarchy. Under this system, folders ask where a note belongs, while links ask how a note relates to other ideas. Especially for longer or more complex projects, that distinction could help turn scattered, disparate readings into a proper argument.

## Comparing the tools

The clearest takeaway was that Joplin and Obsidian are suited to different habits rather than one being universally better. Joplin seems stronger for quick capture, web archiving, daily notes, and users who want a recognizable notebook structure (very easy to learn). Meanwhile, Obsidian seems stronger for long-term research, complex writing, and users who want visible connections across notes. But also, the speaker pointed out that the choice does not have to be mutually exclusive and that someone could capture material quickly in Joplin and use Obsidian for a focused research or writing project.

Also, the web-clipping demonstration showed to me that portability matters in the software you pick. Specifically, Obsidian’s clipper could extract an article’s text and metadata into Markdown rather than preserving only a fragile bookmark. That would be super helpful when a webpage changes or disappears, although clipping still requires citing properly and attention to copyright rules.

## Academic and professional use

As a senior Business major beginning my capstone, I could see a realistic use for either tool. The capstone research I’m doing involves, as expected, things like journal articles, reports, interview notes, datasets, draft sections, and ideas gathered over several months. Thus, Joplin could provide a low-friction place to collect and label those materials. At the same time, Obsidian could be useful later, when I need to connect themes across sources and identify how evidence supports different parts of the argument. Its backlinks could link a concept note to several readings, cases, and draft sections without forcing each item into only one folder.

The same workflow would transfer well into my professional work, too. Business related projects often require combining lots of disparate things, like meeting notes, industry research, competitor information, and drafts produced at different stages. So, a local, portable archive would be useful when revisiting why a decision was made or tracing an idea back to its source.

Ultimately, if I were choosing today, I would probably begin with Joplin because its familiar structure makes adoption more realistic. A tool only helps if I consistently use it obviously. Yet, Obsidian seems more valuable for capstone synthesis, especially once my source collection becomes large enough that relationships among ideas matter more than simple storage. In the end, the workshop helped me see my decisions to work with certain programs (like Google Docs) more critically. And now instead of choosing software only because it is already popular, I now have a better basis for evaluating things like privacy, portability, and the kind of thinking each tool encourages.
